# Almoravid Dynasty

The Almoravids (Arabic **al-Murābiṭūn**, "those who are bound to the ribat") began as a puritanical reformist band of veiled Saharan nomads and ended as the rulers of a million-square-kilometre empire stretching from the banks of the Senegal River to the gates of Zaragoza. In barely a century they unified Morocco, western Algeria, the Saharan trade routes and — for the first time in history — all of Muslim Spain under a single Berber dynasty. Their rigorist Maliki Islam extinguished the heresy of the Barghawata, checked the advance of the Castilian kings, and funded a building programme that gave the western Islamic world some of its enduring monuments. They never claimed the caliphate; they acknowledged the Abbasids of Baghdad and styled themselves *amīr al-muslimīn*, "Commander of the Muslims."

## The Name

Three explanations compete for the origin of the name, and all of them matter:

| Explanation | Source |
| ----------- | ------ |
| **The ribat** — Ibn Yasin, meeting resistance among the Gudala, took followers to raise a makeshift ribat on an offshore island (possibly Tidra, in the Bay of Arguin) | Ibn Abi Zar (13th c.) |
| **The school** — the name came from Waggag ibn Zallu's *Dar al-Murabitin* ("House of the Almoravids"), the ribat in the Sous where Ibn Yasin trained | Qadi Ayyad, Ibn al-Zayyat al-Tadili |
| **Morale** — Ibn Yasin coined it after a bloody battle in the Draa valley c.1054, in the sense of "persevering in the fight" | Ibn 'Idhari (14th c.) |

Al-Bakri, writing in 1068 before their apex, already calls them *al-Murabitun* without explaining why. Whatever the truth, the name was self-chosen — partly, it seems, to forestall any tribal or ethnic identification. Europeans rendered it as *almorávide*; Arabic sources also called them **al-mulathamun**, "the veiled ones", from the *litham* their riders wore against sand and strangers.

## Origins

### The Sahara Before the Empire

The Almoravids trace to several Saharan **Sanhaja** nomadic tribes moving between the Senegal River in the south and the Draa in the north. The founding tribe was the **Lamtuna**, centred on **Awdaghust** (Aoudaghost), the citadel that controlled the trans-Saharan caravan route. Under their king Tinbarutan ibn Usfayshar the Lamtuna had captured or built Awdaghust; after the Sanhaja union collapsed, the city passed to the Ghana Empire and the northern routes to the **Zenata Maghrawa** of Sijilmasa — who also drove the Gazzula and Lamta out of their pastures in the Sous and Draa valleys.

Around 1035 the chieftain Tarsina tried to reunite the desert tribes; his reign lasted under three years.

### A Teacher for the Tribes

Around **1040**, **Yahya ibn Ibrahim**, a Gudala chieftain and brother-in-law of the dead Tarsina, returned from the pilgrimage to Mecca and stopped at **Kairouan**. There he met **Abu Imran al-Fasi**, a Maliki jurist of Fez, and confessed the neglect of Islamic learning among his southern Sanhaja people. On al-Fasi's recommendation Yahya went to the ribat of **Waggag ibn Zallu** in the Sous, and Waggag released to him one of his residents: **Abdallah ibn Yasin**.

Ibn Yasin was a Gazzula Berber, probably a convert rather than a born Muslim — his name has been read as "son of Ya-Sin", the 36th sura, as if he had erased a family past and been reborn of the Book. Al-Bakri alleges his learning was shallow; what is certain is his rigour. He answered disputed arguments with charges of apostasy and punished the slightest deviation. The Gudala expelled him soon after Yahya ibn Ibrahim's death in the 1040s.

The **Lamtuna** received him instead. Their chieftain **Yahya ibn Umar al-Lamtuni** and his brother **Abu Bakr ibn Umar** kept him on a careful leash, while Ibn Yasin supplied the doctrine: conquest as a necessary addendum to Islamicization, tribalism as the obstacle to be destroyed, equality of all Muslims under the Sacred Law. For the Lamtuna leadership, whose ambition was to refound the Sanhaja union and recover lost dominion, the two programs dovetailed perfectly.

By the early 1050s a **triumvirate** led the movement — Abdallah ibn Yasin, Yahya ibn Umar and Abu Bakr ibn Umar — and the movement, now dominated by the Lamtuna, called itself **al-Murabitun**.

### The Two Cities

The first campaign targeted the two poles of the trans-Saharan trade: **Sijilmasa** in the north (Zenata Maghrawa) and **Awdaghust** in the south (Soninke). Both fell in **1054 or 1055**. Mas'ud ibn Wannudin, Sijilmasa's ruler, was killed with other Maghrawa leaders; the army, reported to number 30,000 camel riders, may have been exaggerated. Awdaghust was pillaged and its Muslim population treated harshly on the grounds that they recognized the pagan king of Ghana.

Sijilmasa rebelled as soon as the army left and slaughtered the Lamtuna garrison. The Gudala refused to join the second expedition and went home to the Atlantic desert. Ibn Yasin pressed north regardless.

## History

### The March North

- **1056** — By this year Taroudant and the **Sous valley** were conquered and Maliki law imposed; the army retired to Sijilmasa as its base, and Abu Bakr appointed his cousin **Yusuf ibn Tashfin** to command the garrison there.
- **1055/1056** — Yahya ibn Umar al-Lamtuni was killed fighting the Gudala (some sources give 1057). The first capital was the fortress of **Azuggi** (Azougui), on Jabal Lamtuna about 10 km northwest of modern Atar, in the heartland of the tribe.
- **1058** — Crossing the High Atlas, the Almoravids took **Aghmat**, a prosperous commercial town, and made it the capital.
- **1058/1059** — They met the **Barghawata** confederation of the Atlantic coast. The campaign was bloody: **Abdallah ibn Yasin was killed** at Kurīfalalt (Kurifala). The spiritual founder's death did not stop the war — by **1060** Abu Bakr ibn Umar had conquered the confederation and forced it to orthodox Islam. *See [[Wiki/Events/Almoravid_Conquest]].*
- **c. 1060** — Abu Bakr reached as far as Meknes.

### Zaynab and the Two Empires

Towards **1068** Abu Bakr married **Zaynab an-Nafzawiyyah**, daughter of a wealthy Kairouani merchant settled in Aghmat and widow of Laqut ibn Yusuf al-Maghrawi, the last ruler of the city. When Abu Bakr left for the Sahara to suppress a Gudala revolt (in 1060 or 1071), Zaynab refused to follow; he granted her a divorce and, on his instructions, she married **Yusuf ibn Tashfin** — who, on the advice of her counsel, refused to hand back the north.

The two men met on neutral ground between Aghmat and Marrakesh and divided the realm amicably: Yusuf in the Maghreb, Abu Bakr in the Sahara. Abu Bakr kept the supreme title and Yusuf kept minting coins in his name until Abu Bakr was killed by an arrow wound at Tagant in **November 1087**. The dynasty would spend the next generation and more managing that double inheritance.

### Marrakesh

Abu Bakr founded the new capital soon after — the sources give **1062** (Ibn Abi Zar, Ibn Khaldun), **1078** (al-Idrisi), or **1070** (Ibn 'Idhari), the last being the date most modern historians prefer. The city began as the **Ksar el-Hajjar**, the "Fortress of Stone", built by its founder to house the treasury.

## Expansion

### The Maghreb

| Year | Achievement |
| ---- | ----------- |
| 1069 / 18 Mar 1070 / 1074–75 | **Fez** taken (sources disagree) |
| 1070–1075 | Ibrahim ibn Abu Bakr rules Sijilmasa for his father |
| 1076 | Ibrahim's challenge to Yusuf defused by Mazdali ibn Tilankan |
| 1079 | Banu Ya'la defeated near the Moulaya valley (20,000 men under Mazdali) |
| 1081 | **Oujda** and **Tlemcen** taken; the Maghrawa leader al-Abbas killed |
| 1082 | **Algiers** captured — the easternmost outpost |
| 1083 / 1084 | **Ceuta**, the last Zenata hold on the Strait, falls after a land siege and a Sevillian naval blockade |
| 1104 | Peace treaty with the **Hammadids** fixes the eastern frontier |

At Tlemcen Yusuf founded a new town, **Takrart**, beside the older Agadir; the two merged under the Almohads into the present city.

### Al-Andalus: Invitation and Annexation

The Taifa kingdoms, paying tribute (*parias*) to the Christian kings, finally broke: **Alfonso VI** reached Tarifa in 1083 and took **Toledo** in May 1085. Al-Mu'tamid of Seville convened al-Mutawwakil of Badajoz and Abdallah ibn Buluggin of Granada and sent an embassy to Ibn Tashfin — knowing exactly what it risked. Al-Mu'tamid is said to have remarked: *"Better to pasture camels than to be a swineherd."*

The price was **Algeciras**, surrendered to Yusuf in July 1086; 500 advance troops crossed first, then some 12,000 men. On **23 October 1086** Alfonso VI was routed at **Sagrajas** (Zallaqa), north of Badajoz. Yusuf did not pursue — he returned to Africa, unwilling to be long away from his base.

| Year | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 1088 | Siege of Aledo fails through Taifa disunity |
| 1090 | **Granada** surrenders; Abdallah ibn Buluggin exiled to Aghmat |
| 1091 | **Córdoba** taken; **Seville** surrenders (September) — al-Mu'tamid exiled to Aghmat; **Almería** falls |
| 1091/92 | **Murcia** seized |
| 1094 | **Badajoz** annexed; **Valencia** remains contested — El Cid takes the city 15 June 1094 after a 20-month siege |
| 1097 | Battle of **Consuegra** — Alfonso VI wins; El Cid's son Diego killed |
| 1102 | Christians evacuate **Valencia** (April–May); Almoravids occupy it |
| 1110 | **Zaragoza** taken — the last independent taifa disappears |

By Yusuf's death in **1106** the Almoravids held all of al-Andalus except Zaragoza — but they had not recovered a single metre of land lost to the Christians in the previous century. The Maliki *fuqaha* of Spain, who had blessed the Almoravid jihad and denounced the taifa kings as impious, were the movement's best propaganda.

### The Southern Wing and the Ghana Debate

Abu Bakr made **Azuggi** the capital of the southern Almoravids. Arab tradition holds that they conquered the **Ghana Empire** of the Soninke around **1076–77** — Ibn Khaldun, citing the faqih Shaykh Uthman (1394), records tribute wrung from the Sudan until the rulers of Ghana dwindled away and were absorbed by the Sosso.

Modern Africanists largely reject a military conquest: **Conrad, Fisher and Masonen** argue the story is folklore built on misread Arabic sources, and **Timothy Insoll** notes that the archaeology of ancient Ghana shows no sign of the sudden destruction a conquest would leave. **Dierke Lange** allows political agitation without invasion — Almoravid influence spread by intermarriage with the nobility — while **Sheryl Burkhalter** counters that controlling West African gold so thoroughly implies real political power. Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri records that the Almoravids ended Ibadi Islam at Tadmekka in 1084 and that Abu Bakr "arrived at the mountain of gold".

## State & Government

Under Yusuf ibn Tashfin the western Maghreb was divided into **provinces for the first time** — before that, largely tribal territory — with a developing central government at Marrakesh and key provinces entrusted to allies and relatives. The state was funded by Islamic taxes and by **gold from the south**, but in practice remained dependent on the spoils of each new conquest.

Yusuf acknowledged the **Abbsaid caliphs of Baghdad** as overlords — a symbolic act that enhanced his legitimacy enormously — and took the title ***amīr al-muslimīn***. Ibn 'Idhari dates this to 1073–74; Évariste Lévi-Provençal and Amira Bennison place it later, but certainly by the 1090s, when Abu Bakr ibn al-Arabi found Friday prayers already being given in the Abbasid caliph's name across Yusuf's territories. **They never claimed the caliphate itself.**

Personal security rested on a recruited guard: the ***ḥashm*** of 5,000 black soldiers (***'abid***) and 500 white soldiers (***uluj***, probably of European origin).

## Faith

The Almoravid state was an instrument of **Maliki orthodoxy** imposed on a heterodox Maghreb:

- The Barghawata's Berber Quran and syncretic practices were destroyed on conquest (1058–1060), and orthodox *qadis* appointed.
- Maliki jurists were the dynasty's staunchest allies, in Africa and in Spain alike, praising its jihad and condemning the taifa kings' impiety.
- The **ribat** — the frontier monastery-fortress of the *r-b-ṭ* root — was both institution and ideology: a life bound together in the cause of God.
- Their most formidable theological opponent was **Ibn Hazm** of Córdoba, who attacked them in *al-Fasl fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa*.

The leading jurists of the age served them: **Ibn Rushd al-Jadd** (grandfather of Averroes), chief Maliki jurist under Ali ibn Yusuf; **Abu Bakr ibn al-Arabi**, qadi of Seville; and **Qadi Ayyad**, author of *Kitab al-Shifa*.

## Military

### Organization

Abdallah ibn Yasin imposed punishing discipline for every breach of his laws. Yahya ibn Umar gave the movement its military form:

- **Infantry as the main arm** — javelins in the front rank, pikes behind, formed into a **phalanx**
- **Camelmen and horsemen on the flanks**
- A **flag bearer** ahead whose flag, held upright, meant *stand* and lowered meant *sit*
- Al-Bakri reports they **did not pursue those who fled** before them, did not retreat when outmatched, and preferred death to defeat — characteristics he found unusual in his own time

Their identity was Saharan: the ***litham*** veils that made them *al-mulathamun* to their enemies, and camel-borne mobility that let them cross terrain no Andalusian army could follow.

### The Fleet

Yusuf learned his lesson failing to take Ceuta by land in 1078 and commissioned a fleet. From **Ksar es-Seghir** and **Algeciras** he could move armies across the Strait in a day; he personally crossed six times (1086, 1088, 1090–91, 1094, 1097, 1102). The Almoravids had access to some **18 major ports** — from Tortosa and Dénia to Tangier, Ceuta, Oran and Algiers.

The admiralty followed the Cordoban pattern, seated at **Almería** and secondarily Seville, and was held for the whole period by the **Banu Maymun** clan of Dénia. Their eventual defection to the Almohads handed the new masters the arsenals of Seville and Almería — and with them the ability to take every remaining Almoravid port.

Campaigns ranged wide: 70 ships sent to the Levant in 1105/06 (all lost in a storm), the Atlantic raid on Galicia in 1120, reprisals against Italy with 25 ships in 1118, Calabria in 1122 (Nicotera taken with 17 ships), Patti and Syracuse in 1127, southern Italy again in 1137. The last decades were a slow maritime collapse: the Balearic expedition of 1113–1115 met rising Genoese and Pisan power, Minorca was assaulted in 1146, **Almería was sacked by the Genoese in 1147**, **Lisbon fell to crusaders in 1147**, and **Oran was taken in 1148**.

## Society & Economy

- **Tribes:** the core confederation of **Lamtuna, Gudala and Massufa** Sanhaja, with the Zenata generally hostile and the Masmuda submitted.
- **The desert route:** Ceuta–Fez–Marrakesh–Aghmat–Sijilmasa–Tamdoult–Azougui–Awdaghust–Senegal, terminus of the gold trade.
- **Cities:** Fez, Tlemcen, Aghmat, Marrakesh, Salé (grain out, Sevillian olive oil in), Saltes-Huelva (ironworking and naval metallurgy), Almería (over 800 *tiraz* workshops and the greatest industrial centre in al-Andalus).
- **Paper:** **104 paper mills in Fez** under Yusuf ibn Tashfin alone (al-Manuni).
- **Women:** Zaynab an-Nafzawiyya, described by Ibn 'Idhari as Yusuf's most important political advisor, stands as the most visible figure — but her influence ran through a marriage that was also a state decision.

## Architecture & Arts

The Almoravid period, with the Almohad that followed, is the formative stage of **Moroccan and Moorish architecture**. The Almoravids pulled back from the Andalusi drift towards heavy ornament, restoring balance between proportion and decoration, while importing eastern techniques — above all the **muqarnas**.

| Work | Date | Significance |
| ---- | ---- | ------------ |
| **Ksar el-Hajjar**, Marrakesh | c.1070 | First fortress and treasury of the capital |
| **Great Mosque of Algiers** | c.1097 | Early Almoravid mosque |
| **Almoravid Qubba**, Marrakesh | 12th c. | One of the few surviving Almoravid monuments: carved stucco, complex arches, corner muqarnas cupolas |
| **Al-Qarawiyyin**, Fez (expansion) | 1135 | Central nave holds the **earliest full muqarnas vaulting in the western Islamic world** |
| **Great Mosque of Tlemcen** | 1136 | Ribbed dome before the mihrab — a twelve-pointed star of intersecting ribs, partly pierced to filter light |
| **Walls of Marrakesh** | c.1126 | Rammed earth, built by Ali ibn Yusuf against the Almohad threat; **Bab Doukkala** best preserves the original bent-entrance plan |
| **Riad garden**, Marrakesh | 12th c. | Earliest known Moroccan example of the four-part interior garden |

Muslim, Christian and Jewish artisans from Iberia were brought to Africa after Sagrajas. Textiles, ceramics, calligraphy, manuscript illumination and carved minbars all flourished; the gold dinar minted at Seville in 1116 set the standard that became the Christian **maravedí**.

## Literature & Learning

The political union of Morocco and al-Andalus accelerated cultural exchange — beginning when Yusuf exiled the poet-king **al-Mu'tamid ibn Abbad** first to Tangier and then to Aghmat, where he was buried in 1095.

- **al-Bakri** — *al-Masalik wa'l-Mamalik* (Book of Roads and Kingdoms), the indispensable contemporary source
- **Ibn Hayyan**, **Ibn Bassam** (*Dhakhira*), **al-Fath ibn Khaqan** (*Qala'id al-Iqyan*)
- **Qadi Ayyad** — *Kitab al-Shifa*; **Avempace** (Ibn Bajja)
- **Poetry:** the *muwashshah*, and the vernacular Andalusi *zajal* of **Ibn Quzman**
- Several of the **Seven Saints of Marrakesh** were men of letters of this period

## Decline

The Almohad rebellion begun by **Ibn Tumart** in the 1120s proved fatal. The movement's mountain guerrilla severed the roads to Sijilmassa — the lifeline of trans-Saharan gold — and no Almoravid army could dislodge them from the passes.

| Year | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 1130 | First Almohad descent ends in the disaster of **al-Buhayra** before Marrakesh — but the Almoravid victory bought nothing |
| 1143 | Ali ibn Yusuf dies; succeeded by Tashfin ibn Ali |
| 1144 | The Zenata of western Algeria and elements of the Masufa defect to the Almohads; **Tlemcen** falls |
| March 1145 | Tashfin dies fleeing to Oran |
| 1146 | **Fez** falls after a nine-month siege |
| **1147** | **Marrakesh** falls after an eleven-month siege; **Ishaq ibn Ali** killed |

The final years had also brought a maritime catastrophe — Almería, Tortosa, Lisbon, Oran and the whole Ifriqiyan coast lost within a few years. Muslim maritime power had never looked weaker.

---

## Legacy

- **The first great Berber-led empire of the western Mediterranean**, and the first to unite the Maghreb and al-Andalus politically.
- **Islamization of the Sahara** and the urbanization of the western Maghreb.
- An **architectural vocabulary** — muqarnas vaulting, ribbed domes, bent-entrance gates, the rammed-earth city wall — that the Almohads, Marinids and Saadians all inherited.
- The **gold dinar** that became the Iberian *maravedí*, a coin name that outlived the dynasty by centuries.
- A **southern branch** in the Sahara that persisted for generations after 1147; some oral traditions link its last offshoot to the legendary Ndiadiane Ndiaye, founder of the Jolof Empire.
- In Iberian memory, the *moros* whose veils and discipline haunted the chronicles — and whose fall opened the way for the Almohads.
