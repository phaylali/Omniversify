# Almohad Caliphate

The Almohads — Arabic **al-Muwaḥḥidūn**, "those who profess the unity of God" — were a doctrinal revolution that became an empire. Beginning as a handful of High Atlas tribesmen swearing allegiance to a self-proclaimed Mahdi, they spent eight years in mountain guerrilla warfare, survived the near-annihilation of their leadership in a single afternoon outside Marrakesh, and then, within thirty years, extinguished the Almoravid dynasty, took every port from Tunis to Seville, and proclaimed a caliphate that claimed authority over the whole Muslim West. Their era produced Averroes and Ibn 'Arabī, the Giralda and the Hassan Tower, and a white-bannered military machine that only collapsed after Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212.

## The Name & the Doctrine

*Al-Muwaḥḥidūn* means literally "those who affirm God's unity" — *Almohades* in Spanish. The name was a verdict on their neighbours. Where the Almoravids enforced a rigid Maliki traditionalism, Ibn Tumart preached a **fundamentalist tawhid** that denied the independent existence of God's attributes as incompatible with His unity, and treated the anthropomorphic reading of scripture as polytheism.

| Doctrine | Almohad position |
| -------- | ---------------- |
| **Tawhid** | Absolute transcendence (*tanzih*); no likening God to creation |
| **Imam** | By **knowledge** and appointment, not lineage |
| **Mahdi** | Ibn Tumart claimed to be the Mahdi — but explicitly **denied being a prophet** |
| **Law** | The **Zahiri** school, literalist; hostile to *ijma* (scholarly consensus) |
| **Theology** | **Ash'ari** creed |
| **Dhimma** | **Rejected** — no protected status for Jews and Christians |

Amira Bennison describes the synthesis as "a sophisticated hybrid form of Islam that wove together strands from Hadith science, Zahiri and Shafi'i fiqh, Ghazalian social actions (hisba), and spiritual engagement with Shi'i notions of the imam and mahdi." It was a revolutionary program dressed in the vocabulary of early Islam.

## Origins

### The Traveller

**Ibn Tumart** was a **Masmuda** Berber of the **Hargha** tribe, born in the Atlas country around **1080**. He studied first in Spain, then in **Baghdad**, where he attached himself to the theological school of **al-Ash'ari** and came under the influence of **al-Ghazali**. From these masters he assembled his own system — and identified the enemy: the Almoravids, whose Maliki jurists legitimized a worship he considered corrupt.

Returning to the Maghreb around **1117**, he moved through the cities of Ifriqiya preaching and agitating, raiding wine-shops, denouncing laxity. Expelled from **Béjaïa**, he camped outside at Mellala and received his first disciples — **al-Bashir**, who became his chief strategist, and **Abd al-Mu'min**, a Berber of the Kumīya clan from Tagra in the central Maghreb who would one day succeed him.

### The Two Debates

In **1120** he went to **Fez**, debated the Maliki scholars — and, in the street, assaulted the sister of the Almoravid emir Ali ibn Yusuf because she walked unveiled after the manner of Berber women. Expelled again, he went to **Marrakesh**, tracked Ali ibn Yusuf to a mosque and challenged the emir and his scholars to a formal debate. The scholars pronounced his views blasphemous and urged his execution or imprisonment; the emir, more cautious, merely expelled him.

### The Mahdi

He withdrew to his own people, the Hargha, and to his home village of **Igiliz** in the Sous, living ascetically in a cave and emerging only to preach. **Towards the end of Ramadan, late 1121**, after a sermon reviewing his failure to persuade the Almoravids by argument, Ibn Tumart revealed himself as the true **Mahdi** — divinely guided judge and lawgiver. His audience accepted him. It was, in effect, a declaration of war on the Almoravid state.

On the advice of the Hintata chieftain **Umar al-Hintati**, he abandoned the cave in **1122** and went up into the High Atlas, securing the adherence of the **Hargha, Ganfisa, Gadmiwa, Hintata, Haskura and Hazraja**. Around **1124** he established his base at **Tinmel**, in the valley of the Nfis — an almost impregnable position that became the movement's ***dar al-hijra***, the place of retreat that deliberately echoed the Prophet's flight to Medina.

## Organization

Ibn Tumart organized the Almohads as a commune with a minutely detailed constitution:

- **Ahl ad-dār** — "House of the Mahdi", his own family, at the core
- **Council of Ten** — the Mahdi's privy council of his earliest companions
- **Council of Fifty** — the leading sheikhs of the Masmuda tribes
- ***Ṭalaba* and *huffāẓ*** — preachers and Qur'an reciters, with their own representatives

The military hierarchy was equally exact: the **Hargha** first (though not strictly ethnic — it absorbed "honorary" tribesmen, Abd al-Mu'min among them), then the men of Tinmel, then the other Masmuda tribes in order, then the **'abid** black fighters. Each unit was headed by a ***mohtasib***, divided into early and late adherents each under a ***mizwar***, with ***sakkakin*** (treasurers, mint-masters, tax-collectors) above the ***jund***, and the religious corps — muezzins, *hafidh*, *hizb* — above archers, conscripts and slaves.

**Al-Bashir**, the chief strategist, acted as political commissar, enforcing doctrinal discipline among the tribesmen, often with a heavy hand.

## The War Against the Almoravids

### Eight Years in the Mountains (1124–1130)

For the first eight years the rebellion was pure guerrilla war along the peaks and ravines of the High Atlas. Its real damage was strategic: the roads and passes south of Marrakesh became unsafe, threatening the route to **Sijilmassa**, the gateway to the trans-Saharan gold. Too few men could be pushed through the narrow passes to dislodge the rebels, so the Almoravids built strongholds to confine them — most famously **Tasghîmût**, guarding the approach to Aghmat, which the Almohads took in **1132**.

### Al-Buhayra (1130)

In early **1130** the Almohads finally descended for a sizeable lowland attack. They swept aside an Almoravid column before **Aghmat**, chased the survivors to Marrakesh, and besieged the city for **forty days**. In April or May the garrison sallied out and destroyed them at **al-Buhayra**, the great garden east of the city. Half the Almohad leadership died; the survivors barely scrambled back into the mountains.

**Ibn Tumart died in August 1130.** The movement did not collapse — a sign of the skills of his successor. His death was kept secret for three years, a period the chroniclers called a ***ghayba*** (occultation), which gave **Abd al-Mu'min** time to defeat his rivals and hammer wavering tribes back into line. Three years after his leader's death, he was proclaimed **Caliph**.

## Conquests

### The Overthrow of the Almoravids

| Year | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 1133 | Expansion beyond the Atlas begins |
| 1139 | Operations extended to the **Rif** |
| c.1142 | **Taza**: Abd al-Mu'min founds a citadel (*ribat*) and a Great Mosque — the oldest surviving Almohad mosque |
| 1144 | The **Zenata** of western Algeria and Masufa leaders defect; **Tlemcen** falls |
| March 1145 | **Tashfin ibn Ali** dies trying to escape from Oran |
| 1146 | **Fez** falls after a nine-month siege |
| **1147** | **Marrakesh** falls after an eleven-month siege; the last Almoravid, **Ishaq ibn Ali**, is killed |

### Ifriqiya and the East

In **1151** Abd al-Mu'min launched east — the Normans' conquests along the Ifriqiyan coast gave him both motive and pretext. **Béjaïa** fell in August 1152, the last Hammadid ruler fleeing by sea. The **Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym** raised an army against the advance and were routed at **Sétif** in April 1153.

What followed reshaped the Maghreb: rather than destroy the Arab tribes, Abd al-Mu'min recruited them — keeping some families as hostages in Marrakesh, showering others with gifts and land — and settled them in the **depopulated Atlantic plains of Morocco**. They became the empire's finest cavalry and the instrument of a **rural Arabization** whose descendants, the *ʕroubiya*, still inhabit those plains. **Tunis** was taken by force in **March 1159**; **Mahdia** surrendered in January 1160, the Normans withdrawing to Sicily; Tripoli followed.

In the 1170s–1180s the **Banu Ghaniya** and the Ayyubid commander **Qaraqush** challenged eastern Almohad power; **Ya'qub al-Mansur** reconquered Ifriqiya in 1187–1188. Saladin's request for an Almohad fleet against the crusaders (1189–90) was declined.

### Al-Andalus

Between **1146 and 1173** the Almohads wrested Iberia from the Almoravids and the taifa remnant, transferring the capital of Muslim Spain from **Córdoba to Seville** and treating the peninsula as a province — its emirs crossing the Strait to lead a jihad and then returning to Morocco.

| Year | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 1171 | Great Mosque of Seville begun (its tower, the **Giralda**, erected 1184) |
| 1190–91 | Campaign in southern Portugal recovers ground lost in 1189 |
| **1195** | **Battle of Alarcos** — Almohad victory over Alfonso VIII earns Ya'qub the title *al-Manṣūr* ("the Victorious") |
| **1212** | **Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa** — Muhammad al-Nasir crushed by Castile, Aragon and Navarre: the decisive break |
| 1236 | Córdoba falls to Castile |
| 1248 | **Seville** falls, 22 December, to Ferdinand III |

## Government

Abd al-Mu'min transformed Ibn Tumart's tribal aristocratic movement into a **dynastic state** — and paid for it in rebellion:

- His son **Muhammad** declared heir in 1154; his other children became governors of the provinces as ***sayyids*** ("nobles").
- The traditional Masmuda elites were retained as advisers and commanders, the ***abnā' al-muwaḥḥidūn*** ("Sons of the Almohads").
- The ***huffāẓ*** were converted from Qur'an memorizers into "guardians" — a training school for the elite, learning riding, swimming, archery and a broad education.
- His own **Kumīya** people supplied some 40,000 men and formed the caliphs' bodyguard; the Hilalian Arabs were balanced against the Masmuda sheikhs.
- In 1154–55 two half-brothers of Ibn Tumart, **Abd al-Aziz and Isa**, rebelled in Fez and killed the governor of Marrakesh; Abd al-Mu'min returned, defeated them, and executed everyone involved.

The empire's prestige rested on the **caliphal claim** itself — a title the Almoravids had deliberately refused.

## Military

The Almohad army was mobilized along tribal lines, with shaykhs as de facto commanders and consultees on policy.

**Berbers — the seven Almohad tribes** entitled to pay and to the name: the **Hargha** of Ibn Tumart (few in number), the **Kumīya** of Abd al-Mu'min (peasant farmers and market traders turned first family of the Maghreb), the men of **Tinmel**, the **Hintata**, **Ganfisa**, **Gadmīwa**, **Haskura** and Sanhaja adherents. All other Masmuda were subjects (*raʿīya*).

**Arabs — the Hilalians and Sulayms:** prized as lancers, fighting in the ***karr wa farr*** (attack and retreat) that became their signature. Their pay far exceeded ordinary soldiers:

| Rank | Arab pay | Ordinary pay |
| ---- | -------- | ------------ |
| Horseman, fully equipped | 25 dinars | 10 dinars |
| Horseman, unequipped | 15 dinars | 8 dinars |
| Foot soldier | 7 dinars | 8 / 3 dinars |
| Minor chief | 50 dinars | — |
| Paramount chief | 1000 dinars | — |

**Others:** the ***Ghuzz*** (Aghzaz), Turkish, Armenian and Kurdish adventurers from Ayyubid lands, paid monthly rather than three times a year — the most famous being **Qaraqush**, an Armenian mamluk who switched sides after defeat. **Muhammad al-Nasir** kept a body of **30,000 black soldiers** armed with spears and javelins; **Christian mercenaries** served the later rulers extensively.

## Society, Language & Learning

- **Berber in the liturgy:** the Friday *khuṭba* was delivered in **Arabic and Berber**, the latter called *al-lisān al-gharbī*, "the western tongue". The preacher of al-Qarawiyyin in Fez was replaced because he lacked Berber. Tashelhit is the Berber language best documented for the medieval period.
- **Arabization:** the settlement of the Hilalians in the Moroccan plains, and the recruitment of Arabized elites, set in motion a rural Arabization whose consequences outlasted the dynasty.
- **Paper and books:** **400 paper mills in Fez** under Ya'qub al-Mansur (al-Manuni).
- **Scholars:** **Ibn Tufayl** (physician and confidant of the caliphs), **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** — introduced by Ibn Tufayl and given the caliph's protection — **Ibn 'Arabī**, **Ibn Zuhr**, **Ibn al-Abbar**, **Ibn Amira**, and the poet **Hafsa bint al-Hajj al-Rukuniyya**.
- **Against the Malikis:** the Almohads publicly burned copies of Malik's *Muwatta* and Maliki commentaries. The chief judge **Ibn Maḍāʾ** ordered the banning of all books by non-Zahirites under Abu Ya'qub, and their actual burning under al-Mansur. Yet al-Mansur's embrace of *reason* as a method of validating *tawhid* effectively licensed philosophical rationalism — which is how Averroes could flourish at the very moment the Malikis were being suppressed.
- **White banners**, "for purity of purpose" — and the long dynastic tradition of white that followed with the Marinids and Saadians.

### Non-Muslims

The Almohads **rejected the dhimma** outright. Abd al-Mu'min allowed a seven-month grace period, then forced the urban Jewish and Christian populations of Morocco to convert. Al-Mansur decreed in 1198 that Jews wear dark blue garments with enormous sleeves and an oversized hat — his son changing the colour to yellow. Conversions were largely superficial: **Maimonides** counselled his community to choose outward conformity over martyrdom, and **Abraham ibn Ezra** composed an elegy for the communities destroyed between Spain and the Maghreb. Many fled to Castile, Aragon and Portugal, or east to more tolerant Muslim lands. The reversal came only under **Idris al-Ma'mun** (1229–1232), who renounced the identification of Ibn Tumart as the Mahdi, allowed open Jewish worship in Marrakesh, and even permitted a Christian church there as part of his alliance with Castile.

## Architecture

Along with the Almoravid period that preceded it, the Almohad age is **the formative stage of Moroccan and Moorish architecture**. Built mostly in **rammed earth and brick**, refined to an industrial scale — "construction became an industry on a scale not seen since Roman times" (Felix Arnold) — its restraint of ornament made form, not surface, the carrier of meaning.

| Work | Date | Note |
| ---- | ---- | ---- |
| **Great Mosque of Taza** | 1142 | Oldest surviving Almohad mosque |
| **Kutubiyya Mosque**, Marrakesh | begun 1147, rebuilt before 1195 | Prototype of the Moroccan mosque and minaret |
| **Kasbah of the Udayas**, Rabat | 1150–51 | Built over a destroyed Almoravid ribat |
| **Great Mosque of Seville** | begun 1171 | Its minaret, the **Giralda**, erected 1184 |
| **al-Buḥayra gardens**, Seville | 1171 | Reservoir, orchards and pleasure pavilions |
| **Ribat al-Fath**, Rabat | from 1190s | Unfinished new capital; **Hassan Tower** begun 1191 |
| **Kasbah of Marrakesh** | 1190s | Royal citadel; public entrance **Bab Agnaou** |
| **Torre del Oro**, Seville | 1220–1221 | Defensive tower on the Guadalquivir |
| **Calahorra Tower**, Córdoba | 13th c. | Guards the river and the old bridge |
| **Agdal & Menara**, Marrakesh | 12th c. | Royal garden estates on reservoirs |

Signature elements: the **T-plan** mosque (a wider nave to the *mihrab* crossed by a wider aisle along the *qibla*), **square minarets** with two-tiered shafts of polylobed arch and *darj wa ktaf* motifs, and the **"Almohad arch"** — read by scholars as a physical expression of sura 94, in which God relieves and expands the burdened condition. The great ceremonial gates — **Bab Agnaou**, **Bab er-Rouah**, **Bab Oudaia** — set motifs in concentric semicircles around the arch, framed in a rectangular band; the Marinid and later gateways inherited the formula.

## Decline

### The Shattering (1212–1228)

The defeat at **Las Navas de Tolosa** broke Almohad power but not immediately. Regency, truces and fifteen years of stalemate followed the child caliph Yusuf II's death in 1224. Then came the first internal coup: the vizier **Uthman ibn Jam'i** raised the elderly **Abd al-Wahid I** in Marrakesh, while the late al-Nasir's brothers in al-Andalus proclaimed **Abdallah al-Adil**, who seized Spain and had his rival assassinated — "the pebble that finally broke al-Andalus."

From that fracture came ruin. **Abd Allah al-Bayyasi**, the governor of Jaén, allied with **Ferdinand III of Castile** and seized Córdoba in 1225. Leónese and Portuguese raiders swept through unchecked; a hastily raised militia from Seville was slaughtered outside the city, perhaps 20,000 strong. Al-Adil was murdered in October 1227; **Yahya al-Mu'tasim** took Marrakesh, while the Andalusian branch proclaimed **Idris al-Ma'mun** in Seville. In **October 1228**, al-Ma'mun bought a truce from Ferdinand III for 300,000 maravedis, marched the remnant of the Almohad army across the Strait, and abandoned Spain — the end of the Almohad era in Iberia.

The **Nasrids** rose in Granada; **Ibn Hud** and local strongmen filled the vacuum. In a single generation the Christian advance took Mérida and Badajoz (1230), Mallorca (1230), Beja (1234), **Córdoba (1236)**, Valencia (1238), Silves (1242), Murcia (1243), Jaén (1246), Alicante (1248) and **Seville (22 December 1248)**. Granada alone survived — for another 250 years.

### Collapse in Africa

In the Maghreb the Almohads were not overthrown by a rival religion, as they had overthrown the Almoravids, but bled away by tribal revolt. The **Banu Marin** — Zenata adventurers once recruited by Abd al-Mu'min — rose from **1215** and progressively took the north, imposing suzerainty by 1248. The last of the line, **Idris al-Wathiq**, was reduced to Marrakesh, where a slave murdered him in **1269**. The Marinids seized the city, and Almohad rule in the western Maghreb was over.

## Legacy

- **An architectural standard:** the Almohad mosque, minaret, gate and city wall became the template for the Marinids, the Nasrids of Granada and the Saadians — and, through them, for Moroccan and Andalusi building to the present.
- **Averroism:** the philosophy the Almohads protected — Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle — became the channel through which Aristotle reached **Thomas Aquinas** and the Latin West, and a counterpart for **Maimonides**, Averroes's own contemporary.
- **Arabization:** the Hilalian settlement of the Atlantic plains reshaped the language and tribal structure of Morocco permanently.
- **The white banner** as dynastic colour for Morocco's later sultanates.
- **A ruined ideal:** the empire that began by abolishing *dhimma* status ended with a caliph permitting a Christian church in Marrakesh — the doctrine's long arc from revolution to realpolitik.
