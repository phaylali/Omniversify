# Siege of Mazagan (1769)

The **Siege of Mazagan (1769)** — **early 1769 to 11 March 1769** — was the last engagement of the **354-year Moroccan–Portuguese conflicts** and the end of Portugal's presence in Morocco. The **'Alawite Sultan Mohammed ben Abdallah (Mohammed III)**, refusing to acknowledge Portuguese rule over his coast, invested the fortress with **70,000 men and 35 cannon** during **Ramadan**, and after a bombardment of **2,000 round shot** forced the evacuation of **Portugal's last square in Morocco**.

## Background

By 1769 Mazagan — built as a superfortress in **1541–42** — was Portugal's **last possession in Morocco**. **Ceuta** remained Spanish since 1640, **Tangier** had been ceded to England 1661, **Safi and Azamor** abandoned 1541. The garrison of **592**, though complete on paper, reflected Lisbon's already-weighed decision to abandon — José I, advised by the **Marquês de Pombal**, had quietly reduced the Moroccan establishment for six years (Silva 2004:173).

The walls — **275 × 200 m rectangle, five bastions, 12 m thick scarps, 20 × 3 m wet ditch with buxas** — remained formidable, but without relief the 592 could not man the **69 embrasures**, the covered way (Q), the revelim (P) and the 38-ha outer field at once.

---

## The Siege

Sultan **Mohammed ben Abdallah** arrived in Ramadan with the 'Alawite *mhalla*, **35 cannon prepared and 2,000 round shot fired into the city**, inflicting heavy casualties and damage. The garrison wrote to **King José I** asking aid. **José ordered evacuation — take everyone and their families**. The inhabitants first refused — "their ancestors had sacrificed themselves to keep it" — then complied and asked the Sultan for **three days to evacuate**. Mohammed accepted **on condition they leave everything behind** (an-Nasiri VIII:37).

On **1 February** a squadron under **captain of sea and war Bernardo Ramires Esquível** sailed from Lisbon to lift the population (Pereira 2011:97–99): the **54-gun ship of the line *Nossa Senhora de Belém e São José*** (flagship, Esquível), frigates ***Nossa Senhora da Nazaré* and *da Guia***, charruas ***Nossa Senhora das Mercês* and *Santa Ana e São Joaquim***, and **five yachts**.

During the three days the Portuguese **burned furniture and bedding, killed livestock and broke tools** (an-Nasiri:37), then — **violating the agreement** (Carlos Nunes Silva) — **mined the bastions with ~40 barrels of gunpowder in each and the main entrance**, burying charges. **Embarkation completed 11 March; the last off was Governor Dinis Gregório**. Left behind, by tradition, was a man called **"Pedro"**, said to have **fired the train as Moroccans entered, killing ~5,000** (an-Nasiri:37; Silva). The **Governor's Bastion and a long curtain of the main rampart were devastated**.

## Aftermath

The squadron anchored at **Lisbon 21–24 March**; the evacuees were fed at royal expense "with dinner and supper" but **housed miserably; more than three hundred died in hospitals** (Pereira 2011). The **survivors were shipped to Brazil** where in **1773 they founded Vila Nova de Mazagão** in the interior of **Amapá, State of Grão-Pará and Rio Negro** (today **Mazagão Velho**).

The city lay **uninhabited nearly half a century as *El-Mahdouma* (the Ruined)** (UNESCO nomination 2004). Early in the 19th century **Sultan Moulay Abd al-Rahman** ordered the **Pasha to rebuild the destroyed portions (in a slightly different bond), build a mosque, and rehabilitate the city**; **in 1820 the name Mazagan was banned and the city became *El-Jadida* (the New)** (FAR 128–133; Starforts: Mazagan). The **minaret reused the old Torre de Rebate**. Under the **French Protectorate** the quarter became the **Cité Portugaise**, the moat was filled.

In **2004 the Portuguese City of Mazagan was inscribed UNESCO World Heritage** — "an outstanding example of the interchange of influences between European and Moroccan cultures" — and today ~3,700 live inside the walls.
